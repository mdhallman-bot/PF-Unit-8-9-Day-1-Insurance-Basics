Risk-management strategies

Financial risk is the chance of losing money. You can avoid an activity, reduce the chance or size of a loss, retain a manageable loss yourself, or transfer/share some financial responsibility with insurance. Insurance does not prevent an accident; it limits specified financial losses.

StrategyWhat you doExample
AvoidDo not take a particular riskChoose not to skydive
ReduceLower the likelihood or harmWear a safety-certified bicycle helmet
Retain / acceptPay a manageable loss yourselfPay for a minor scratch
Transfer / sharePurchase protection against specified lossesBuy insurance for a major hospital cost

REAL-WORLD PRACTICE

3. A normal year using health insurance

Why would an insurer help pay for preventive care? Some screenings, vaccinations and check-ups can identify problems earlier and help people stay well. Preventive coverage is also required for many services under federal rules. Earlier care may sometimes avoid more expensive complications, but prevention is not a promise that every visit or test is free. Check official preventive-care guidance.

How to use coverage: Before a nonemergency visit, look for your plan’s network directory, call the number on the insurance card, or ask the clinic to verify your specific plan. Ask what the appointment is billed as and whether a copay, deductible, coinsurance or other charge applies.

What does health insurance cost? A preview for Day 2

In a 2025 nationwide employer survey, workers paid about $120/month on average toward single-person job-based health coverage. The total premium averaged $9,325/year (about $777/month), with employers typically paying much of it. These are averages across covered employees of different ages—not prices for a particular 20-year-old. For a quick sense of scale, fictional employer-plan employee contributions might be $0, $120, or $300 per month; these are examples, not a market price range or a promise of coverage. Real costs can be far more or less and differ through a parent, employer, Medicaid, or a Marketplace plan. Premiums are separate from medical bills. Next class you’ll compare realistic quotes and the costs of actually using coverage. 2025 KFF source.

Casey is due for a routine dental cleaning. Preventive cleanings are often covered under a separate dental plan, subject to its frequency and network rules. Adult dental care is not automatically included with regular medical coverage.

Urgent care or telehealth

A standalone urgent care clinic treats many same-day problems that are not immediately life-threatening. Some primary care offices offer same-day or cancellation appointments; many plans also offer telehealth or a nurse advice line by phone or video. Coverage, location and costs vary. For severe or potentially life-threatening symptoms, seek emergency care rather than waiting for routine appointments.

Before Casey’s emergency: Why can the same service have different prices?

1. A hospital's listed charges are not necessarily the amount collected. Hospitals may publish high gross charges, negotiated rates accepted from insurance plans, and discounted cash prices for people paying themselves. These numbers serve different purposes. A gross charge is not automatically a patient's final debt.

2. What a patient owes depends on the plan. Even for the same treatment, two people may owe different amounts because insurer-negotiated prices, remaining deductibles, copays, coinsurance, covered services, and annual out-of-pocket limits differ. A provider's network status can matter too.

3. Uninsured patients can ask questions too. A self-paying patient may be eligible for a cash discount, a hospital financial-assistance policy, or a payment arrangement. They should request an itemized bill or estimate and ask what discounts apply. Neither the highest gross charge nor an insurer's private rate automatically determines what they must pay.

PHASE 2 · INDEPENDENT SIMULATION

4. The unexpected hospital stay

Casey’s story — a normal morning turns serious

On Monday morning, Casey, 23, feels fine and heads to work. By lunchtime, an uncomfortable ache has developed in the abdomen. Casey considers waiting, but the pain gets worse. Casey calls the primary care provider (the regular doctor or clinic that manages everyday health needs). Some offices reserve same-day appointments, although availability is not guaranteed.

The office cannot see Casey right away, but offers a nurse advice line by phone. The nurse asks about symptoms and recommends prompt evaluation. Casey checks the insurance card for an in-network urgent care center and learns that urgent care may handle many non-life-threatening problems, but it may refer people with concerning symptoms to an emergency department. A telehealth visit by phone/video may be useful for some conditions, but it cannot replace an in-person exam or surgery.

Casey goes to an urgent care clinic for an evaluation. Because the abdominal pain is worsening and the clinician suspects a condition requiring hospital care, Casey is directed to the emergency department. Tests identify appendicitis. Casey needs surgery and spends two nights in the hospital.

What is the “allowed amount”?

It is the amount the insurance plan recognizes for covered care under its contract or payment rules. Think of it as the agreed price used to divide the covered cost between the insurer and patient—not the hospital’s highest list price and not the amount Casey personally owes. The hospital bills $42,000; in this model, the hospital’s agreement with the plan reduces the recognized price to $21,000. The difference, $21,000, is a contractual adjustment the in-network provider cannot simply bill to Casey for these covered services. The plan then applies Casey’s deductible and coinsurance to the allowed $21,000.

Compare: $42,000 billed → $21,000 allowed → $5,800 patient responsibility + $15,200 insurance payment. The last two numbers are calculated in Stage B.

Follow the path, not just the largest number: The hospital begins with $42,000 in fictional gross charges. For Casey's covered in-network claim, the insurance plan recognizes $21,000, and the plan rules divide that amount between patient and insurer. A person paying cash might instead ask about the hospital's $27,000 published self-pay price and additional assistance. Neither $21,000 nor $27,000 is automatically the final amount owed.

Stage C — Simplified Explanation of Benefits (EOB)

An Explanation of Benefits (EOB) is an insurance statement showing the services submitted by providers, what they charged, the allowed amounts, plan adjustments, what insurance paid, and the patient’s estimated responsibility. It helps patients spot mistakes and compare later provider bills. An EOB is not itself a bill. Stage D — Medication and coverage

After discharge, a doctor prescribes medication. Casey's plan's formulary shows a covered generic medicine with a $12 copay. A brand-name option would cost Casey $65 under the modeled plan. Assume the doctor says the generic is an appropriate alternative.

Stage E — An unusual charge

An out-of-network anesthesiology bill arrives related to Casey's emergency treatment at an in-network hospital. Under federal No Surprises Act protections, covered emergency care generally cannot be balance billed beyond applicable in-network cost-sharing. Specific facts and exceptions matter; do not assume every out-of-network bill is valid.
