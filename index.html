PHASE 2 · INDEPENDENT SIMULATION

4. The unexpected hospital stay

Fictional case: Casey, 23, has emergency appendicitis, needs surgery, and stays in the hospital for two nights. All services in the calculation below are assumed covered and in-network; this simplified example is not a real quote or an actual insurance contract.

Stage A — The bill

Hospital's billed chargesAmount
Emergency assessment and tests$7,200
Surgery, anesthesia, operating room$20,500
Two nights in hospital, medications, monitoring$14,300

Casey's insurer negotiates an allowed amount of $21,000 for these covered services.

Compare three different prices (fictional illustration)
Hospital gross billed chargesPublished self-pay/cash priceInsurer-negotiated allowed amount
$42,000$27,000$21,000

These are not three bills for the same patient. The final amount owed depends on coverage, discounts, and financial assistance.

Important: The difference is not automatically a patient bill. Here, assume the hospital accepts the allowed amount as full payment for these covered in-network services.

Stage D — Medication and coverage

After discharge, a doctor prescribes medication. Casey's plan's formulary shows a covered generic medicine with a $12 copay. A brand-name option would cost Casey $65 under the modeled plan. Assume the doctor says the generic is an appropriate alternative.

Stage E — An unusual charge

An out-of-network anesthesiology bill arrives related to Casey's emergency treatment at an in-network hospital. Under federal No Surprises Act protections, covered emergency care generally cannot be balance billed beyond applicable in-network cost-sharing. Specific facts and exceptions matter; do not assume every out-of-network bill is valid.

Source: CMS: Surprise medical bill rights.
